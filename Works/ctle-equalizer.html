45nm CTLE for a 12.5 Gb/s SerDes Link

CTLE Complete · DFE In Progress Github Repository →

A transistor-level continuous-time linear equalizer, designed from first principles in Cadence Virtuoso and tested against two real, industry-sourced backplane channel models, one of which it fixed, and one of which it made measurably worse, in a way that turned out to be the most interesting part of the project!

Process 45nm CMOS

Context & Objective

This project began with a deep dive into differential pairs, and from there into differential signaling, current sources, and active loads. Somewhere along the way I got pulled toward the CTLE, partly for the eye diagrams, partly for everything underneath them. I picked an arbitrary Nyquist rate of 6.25 GHz, close to today's standards, putting the data rate at 12.5 Gb/s. At this rate, a backplane channel doesn't pass a signal so much as interrogate it. Skin effect, dielectric loss, and impedance discontinuities strip away all the high-frequency content that carries bit transitions, closing the eye before a CDR ever samples it. A CTLE sits at the receiver's front end and does the opposite: it boosts back what the channel took away!

The design outlined above followed the methodology of Ankit Jain's UIUC thesis on 65nm equalizer design, supported by the math from Sam Palermo's Lectures on High-Speed Links. For the project, I adapted to 45nm technology and to real, publicly available 12.5 Gb/s backplane channel models.

The Channel

Rather than design against a textbook low-pass model, I sourced real 4-port S-parameter data from a public IEEE 802.3ck contribution: two 12-inch, Megtron-7 backplane channels, identical in length and dielectric, differing only in connector architecture. One uses a traditional, non-backdrilled via structure. The other uses a Direct-Plug Orthogonal connector.

Parsing the Touchstone files and converting to differential insertion loss (SDD21) in Python revealed something the datasheet loss figure alone does not: both channels attenuate by almost exactly the same amount at 6.25 GHz Nyquist, roughly 10 dB. But the traditional channel's loss curve is dominated by via-stub resonance, deep periodic notches reaching −42 dB, averaging nearly 8 dB of ripple across the band. The orthogonal channel's curve, by contrast, is almost perfectly smooth, averaging just 0.03 dB of ripple over the same range. This is when the "weird" of the project started kicking in. Both had about the same loss, but wildly different shapes!

Sizing Against Real Silicon

The classic square-law hand calculation for transconductance, gm = 2·Id/Vov, is a long-channel approximation. At 45nm, it overestimates gm by roughly 40% against what the transistor actually delivers. Rather than carry that error through the whole design, I characterized the real device directly: a DC sweep in Virtuoso, gm/Id and current density extracted at a chosen moderate-inversion operating point, and every downstream calculation built from that measured data instead of an idealized formula.

Even with real device data, the ideal peaking-gain equation, gain approaching gm*RL, consistently overshot what the simulator actually produced. Tracing the gap down to its source revealed two physical effects the textbook formula omits: finite transistor output resistance (Ro) loading down the effective load, and body effect acting as an unaccounted second degeneration path. A single stage topped out around 6 dB of peaking gain against an ideal target of 9 to 11. The fix, precedented directly in a second thesis on 45nm video-rate equalizers, was to cascade two identical stages rather than force one stage past its real physical ceiling. Initially, I had started with the traditional backplane, and hence arrived at a gain of around +9.0018 dB, but because of what I would find out later, I decided to switch to orthogonal backplane channel, with a gain of +10.149 dB.

Proving It — Both Ways

A bode plot that peaks in the right place is not proof an equalizer works. The real test is a transient simulation: a PRBS bitstream driven through the actual channel S-parameters, through the equalizer, and measured as an eye diagram. I ran the identical CTLE design against both channels and got two opposite answers.

On the orthogonal channel, the eye height crosses from negative, meaning the two logic levels statistically overlap, to a clean positive value: a closed link becomes an open one. On the traditional channel, gain and edge speed both improve by every individual metric, and the eye still ends up more closed than it started. Same circuit, nearly identical raw loss on both channels, but opposite outcomes!

Key Insights

A Linear Equalizer Amplifies the Problem, Not Just the Signal

The traditional channel's eye got worse, not just insufficiently better, after equalization. A CTLE boosts everything in its passband, including reflection-driven interference, not only the wanted signal. When a channel's closure comes from resonance rather than smooth attenuation, adding gain amplifies the interference right along with the data. That is not a tuning failure. It is the reason decision feedback equalizers, which act on decision history rather than on frequency content, exist as the next stage in a real receiver chain.

Theory Will Only Take You So Far!

Every ideal formula in this design, gm from square law, peak gain from gm times RL, needed correction once checked against simulated silicon. Output resistance, body effect, and pole proximity are not edge cases; they are the default behavior of a real 45nm device. The hand calculation's real job was never to be exact. It was to get close enough that a parametric sweep had something sensible to converge from.

Trust the Simulator Over the Datasheet Number

A cached, stale simulation once produced a falsely clean eye on the resonant channel, one that looked like good news and wasn't. Catching it meant reading the simulator's own log rather than trusting a plot that happened to look right. The corrected result held up the original, harder conclusion. In a field where a subtly wrong result looks exactly like a right one, the habit of re-verifying against the raw log is not optional.

What's Next

The traditional channel's result is not a dead end, but a handoff. A decision feedback equalizer (DFE) corrects intersymbol interference based on what was actually just received, regardless of whether that interference came from attenuation or reflection. I am currently building a DFE aimed specifically at recovering the traditional channel's eye, to possibly try and open it.
